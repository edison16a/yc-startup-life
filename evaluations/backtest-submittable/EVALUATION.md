## General assessment

Submittable, formerly Submishmash, sells a hosted workflow for receiving and reviewing manuscript and contest submissions. It is capital efficient and already has paying customers, which is rare among applicants, but the market of literary magazines is small and mostly nonprofit. The reported Summer 2012 acceptance rate is about 2 percent, so even with traction the chance of getting in is low. If accepted, the most likely path is a small seed or none, then slow revenue growth, and the upside depends entirely on expanding to larger segments such as contests, grants and universities. Analogs in niche contest and nonprofit tooling mostly stayed small.

## Round by round: where and why it fails

### Getting into YC: 5.0%

**How we got the number.** Start from the reported Summer 2012 acceptance rate of about 2 percent (TechCrunch, May 2012, which called it the most selective class yet). Adjust upward by about 2.5x because the company already has paying customers and a claimed 2,000 plus organizations, a product live for two years and sells a boring but real need, which Graham favours. Adjust slightly down for a tiny addressable market (literary magazines) and no stated technical or venture scale story. Net about 5 percent, range 2 to 10 percent.

**Why it fails here.** YC would most plausibly say the market is too small and too slow: literary magazines are mostly nonprofit and have little money, so a venture return needs a leap into broader submission workflows such as contests, grants, universities and HR, which the application does not yet show. The product is also easy to copy by an established competitor or a small team, and the company is already growing without YC, so partners may see little need for the program.

## Pros

- **Real paying customers and revenue before YC**: Self reported over 2,000 publishers and universities and a live product for two years. (https://htmlgiant.com/tag/submishmash/)
- **Capital efficient boring B2B workflow with a clear pain**: Replaces postal and email intake, a documented shift among literary magazines. (https://www.pw.org/content/writers_editors_say_adios_sases?cmnt_all=1)

## Roadmap

### Get into YC

Show paying customers and growth beyond literary magazines.

1. Publish monthly revenue and active organizations in the application
2. Add a second segment such as contests or grants and show first customers

You know it worked when: Recurring revenue and at least a few non literary customers.

### Reach Seed

Convert YC and revenue into a seed or stay profitable.

1. Raise a small seed on revenue growth
2. Hire a first salesperson and build integrations and payments depth

You know it worked when: Net revenue retention above 100 percent and a consistent monthly growth rate.

## Assumptions and limits

- Description is based only on the 2010 to 2012 facts: product, location, self reported customer count and the 2 percent acceptance rate. Later changes in scope and name are ignored.
- Team background and revenue amounts are unknown as of 2012 and assumed modest.
- The company row description in companies.csv is from today and was not used.
- B2B 2005-2011 cohort (n=141) used for anchors since the SaaS narrow cohort had only 25; calibration-only cohort has 36 companies with similar numbers (seed 88%, A 67%, B 50%, C 40%).
- Valuations for 2012 are scarce in the data, so bands rely on the benchmarks and general 2012 climate.
