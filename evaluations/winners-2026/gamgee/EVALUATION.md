## General assessment

Gamgee turns one dog's treatment into a service for personalised mRNA vaccines for pets. Dog owners pay for cancer care and FidoCure shows investors fund the category, so a small seed is plausible, helped by a strong story. The hard parts are repeatable results, per case cost, regulation and manufacturing, and a Series A needs trial data. Analogs split between FidoCure, which raised an A, and mRNA or grant funded companies that stayed small. The healthcare cohort is slow to seed, so the one year seed estimate is lower than the cohort anchor.

## Round by round: where and why it fails

### Getting into YC: 2.0%

**How we got the number.** Test assumes the company was accepted, so this is only a note. Prior about 1 percent (0.6 to 2 percent third party range). Up for a vivid personal origin and a technical founder story with a real treated case, down for biotech capital need, a regulatory path that YC rarely funds and a team of only two.

**Why it fails here.** YC would most plausibly pass because one treated dog is not a repeatable business: the evidence is a single case, the vaccine was made with university scientists and the founder's wet lab and veterinary credentials are unclear. Biotech with long timelines is also a poor fit for a three month batch.

## Pros

## Roadmap

### Get into YC

Show repeatable evidence beyond a single dog

1. Treat two or three more dogs under a trial protocol
2. Document tumour response and costs

### Reach Seed

2. Sign a veterinary oncology referral partner

### Reach Series A

Prove a standardised vaccine process

1. Cut turnaround and cost per vaccine
2. Start a controlled study with a university

You know it worked when: A controlled trial with positive response data
